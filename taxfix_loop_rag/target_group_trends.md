# Target Group Trends — Deep Research Cards

Synthetic research output for the Taxfix Loop demo. Every card below is an invented research artefact built to be plausible, not a real study. Sources are either generic observations labelled as synthetic demo assumptions or real source *types* that a team would verify before quoting. Nothing here is tax advice.

## How to Read These Cards

Each H2 section is one research card and one retrieval chunk. The shape is always the same: a trend name, then **Insight**, **Implication for the target group**, **Product opportunity**, **Confidence**, and **Source**.

Confidence is the research agent's own self-assessment, not a statistical claim. `high` means the pattern is widely observed and safe to design around. `medium` means it is plausible but could shift. `low` means the team should test it with real users before building on it. A low-confidence card must never be turned into a hard product rule.

The target group is Alex: 31, employed as a software developer, occasionally freelancing in UX and web work, anxious about taxes and inclined to avoid them. Cards describe the group around Alex, not Alex alone. When a card and a live user disagree, the live user wins.

## Multiple Income Streams Are Now Normal

**Insight:** For many younger professionals, a single salary is no longer the whole picture. A side project, a weekend freelance invoice, a small online sale, or a creator payout may sit alongside the main job. These extras are often small and irregular.

**Implication for the target group:** Alex may not think of himself as "self-employed". The side income feels like a hobby until a document or a threshold makes it feel official. That gap between self-image and paperwork is where confusion starts.

**Product opportunity:** Treat every extra income type as a first-class, nameable thing in the product. Ask "did anything else bring in money this year?" in plain words, without the label "self-employment".

**Confidence:** high

**Source:** Synthetic demo assumption, consistent with widely reported growth in platform and freelance work among younger professionals. OECD/Statistisches Bundesamt style public reporting — verify before citing.

## Avoidance Driven by Fear of Getting It Wrong

**Insight:** Avoidance is usually not laziness. It is risk management. People delay tasks when the cost of an error feels high and the rules feel opaque. Tax paperwork scores badly on both counts.

**Implication for the target group:** Alex does not need more urgency. Pressure increases avoidance. He needs the first step to be small enough that being wrong is cheap and reversible.

**Product opportunity:** Replace deadlines with small, finishable actions. Make "we can change this later" an explicit, visible promise on every editable field.

**Confidence:** high

**Source:** Synthetic demo assumption, consistent with general behavioural research on task avoidance under uncertainty. Verify framing before citing.

## Desire for Control and Approval

**Insight:** Anxious users want two things at once: to feel in charge, and to be told they are doing it right. They will re-read, re-check, and second-guess.

**Implication for the target group:** Alex may re-open the same screen several times without making progress. Reassurance that arrives as a vague "looks good" does not help and can feel patronising.

**Product opportunity:** Show a visible checklist state, what is done, what is missing, and what is genuinely optional. Approval should be specific: "this section is complete" beats "great job".

**Confidence:** medium

**Source:** Synthetic demo assumption. Test the wording with real users, since reassurance can read as pressure if overdone.

## Expectation of Personalised Digital Finance Products

**Insight:** People in this group already use apps that know their context: banking, budgeting, fitness, streaming. They expect the product to remember what they told it and to stop asking.

**Implication for the target group:** Repeating a question Alex already answered reads as incompetence and erodes trust fast. Generic tips that ignore his freelance side income feel like they were written for someone else.

**Product opportunity:** Persist every answer and reuse it. Reference the user's own facts back to them: "you told us in March that you worked from home two days a week."

**Confidence:** high

**Source:** Synthetic demo assumption, consistent with general expectations set by consumer finance apps.

## Mobile-First, Short Attention

**Insight:** This group starts financial admin on a phone, often in a spare two minutes. Sessions are short, interrupted, and rarely finished in one go.

**Implication for the target group:** If a task cannot be paused and resumed without losing place, Alex will not come back to it. Long forms on a small screen convert badly.

**Product opportunity:** Design every step to be completable in under three minutes and savable mid-way. Progress must survive closing the app.

**Confidence:** high

**Source:** Synthetic demo assumption, consistent with broad mobile usage reporting for consumer apps. Verify current device statistics before quoting numbers.

## Distrust of Financial Jargon

**Insight:** Terms like "Werbungskosten", "Sonderausgaben", or "Lohnsteuerbescheinigung" signal "this is not for you" to non-experts. The jargon itself is a barrier, separate from the underlying task difficulty.

**Implication for the target group:** Alex may understand the concept perfectly but still freeze at the word. He may also assume a document is irrelevant because he cannot decode its title.

**Product opportunity:** Lead with the everyday meaning, then optionally reveal the official term as a small secondary label. Never require the official word to make progress.

**Confidence:** high

**Source:** Synthetic demo assumption. Plain-language guidance from public tax authorities is a plausible source type — verify before citing.

## Year-Round Versus Once-a-Year Behaviour

**Insight:** Tax behaviour today is seasonal and deadline-shaped. Effort spikes, then disappears for eleven months. Meanwhile the events that matter for tax happen all year.

**Implication for the target group:** By the time Alex sits down to file, the details are gone: which months he worked from home, what the laptop cost, when the invoice was paid. Memory becomes the bottleneck.

**Product opportunity:** Capture small facts at the moment they happen, in one tap, and store them for later. The product's job in month three is capture, not filing.

**Confidence:** high

**Source:** Synthetic demo assumption, plus OECD/Statistisches Bundesamt style public reporting on filing behaviour — verify before citing.

## Life Events as Tax Triggers

**Insight:** People do not become interested in tax. They get pulled in by events: marriage, a move, a child, a new job, a first invoice. Each event quietly changes what may be relevant.

**Implication for the target group:** Alex will not search for "tax changes after marriage". He will search for "we just got married, what now". The product must meet the life event, not the tax category.

**Product opportunity:** Build a small library of life-event entry points and surface them at the moment the user mentions the event, in the user's own words.

**Confidence:** medium

**Source:** Synthetic demo assumption. Life-event framing is common in public administration service design — verify before citing.

## Work-From-Home Normalisation

**Insight:** Hybrid work has made the home desk ordinary. For many employees, part of the week happens at home, sometimes with a dedicated room and sometimes from a kitchen table.

**Implication for the target group:** Alex probably has a rough sense that home office "counts" but no idea what that means for paperwork, what he is allowed to do, or what he must prove.

**Product opportunity:** Ask about the *arrangement*, not the deduction: how many days, is there a separate room, who pays the internet. Offer to record it and revisit later.

**Confidence:** medium

**Source:** Synthetic demo assumption, consistent with widely reported hybrid-work patterns. Verify current figures before citing.

## AI Trust Needs Visible Uncertainty

**Insight:** People will accept an AI assistant being unsure. They react badly to an assistant that is confidently wrong, especially about money. Trust is built by visible honesty, not by fluent answers.

**Implication for the target group:** If the assistant invents a rule, Alex may act on it and lose money or sleep. One confident mistake can end the relationship with the product.

**Product opportunity:** Make uncertainty a designed, visible state: "I'm not certain about this — here is what it depends on." Attach a confidence marker and a clear path to a human.

**Confidence:** high

**Source:** Synthetic demo assumption, consistent with published work on user trust in AI systems. Verify the specific study before citing.
